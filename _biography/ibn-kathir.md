### Biography of Hafiz Ibn Kathir (May Allah have mercy on him)

#### His Era:

Ibn Kathir (may Allah have mercy on him) lived during the majority of the eighth century AH (701–774 AH / 1301–1373 CE), a period when the Bahri Mamluks[^1] ruled Egypt and the Levant. This era was a continuation of monumental events that swept through the Islamic world, including the Crusades (490–690 AH)[^2], the fall of Baghdad in 656 AH[^3], and fierce power struggles among Muslim rulers, which led to the deposition, execution, or imprisonment of many of them. Al-Suyuti mentions in his book *Husn al-Muhadarah* that King Al-Ala'in Kujuk[^4] was pledged allegiance after his brother was deposed, exiled to Qus[^5] along with his other brothers, and given the title *Al-Malik al-Ashraf* at under six years of age. He ruled for five months before being deposed and imprisoned until his death. He was succeeded by his brother Shihab al-Din Ahmad, titled *Al-Malik al-Nasir*, who remained in power for forty days before being deposed in 743 AH (though some say early 745 AH). He was followed by his brother Imad al-Din Ismail, titled *Al-Malik al-Salih*, who ruled until his death on the 4th of Rabi' al-Akhir in 746 AH at around twenty years of age[^6].

The author of *Khitat al-Sham* mentioned that in the year 742 AH, political upheavals among kings and deputies and widespread instability occurred to an extent not seen for hundreds of years[^7]. Perhaps the weakening of religious commitment, extravagance, wastefulness, and sectarian tensions between Ahl al-Sunnah and the Rafidah were causes of this political turbulence, which inevitably impacted social life. Consequently, many famines occurred, alongside the widespread outbreak of diseases, epidemics, and massive loss of life among the people[^8].

Despite this, the period witnessed a remarkable scientific renaissance characterized by the proliferation of schools and educational institutions[^9]. Knowledge spread, authorship in various sciences flourished, and numerous "independent legal scholars" (*mujtahids*) and master memorizers emerged in diverse fields—such as Ibn Taymiyyah[^10], Al-Hafiz al-Dhahabi, Al-Hafiz al-Mizzi, Al-Birzali, Ibn al-Qayyim, Ibn Kathir, and others.

Perhaps the most important reasons for this scientific renaissance and intellectual activity were:

1. The competition among Mamluk emirs in encouraging the sciences, drawing scholars close to them, and bestowing generous gifts upon them.
2. The abundance of religious endowments (*awqaf*) established by rulers, emirs, and the general public for mosques, schools, and hospices (*ribats*).
3. The awakening of Islamic public opinion following the trials and painful events that befell the Islamic nation.
4. The presence of exceptional scholars who reached the rank of renewal (*tajdid*) and independent reasoning (*ijtihad*) during this period. This era impacted intellectual freedom regarding research and debate on scholarly matters, helping to eradicate blind adherence to specific schools of jurisprudence (*taqlid*), which represented intellectual stagnation and a weakening of intellectual freedom[^11].

Thus, the era in which Ibn Kathir lived had a profound impact on his upbringing, his scholarly formation, his intellectual methodology, his preaching and reformist influence, and the legacy of works he left behind in exegesis (*tafsir*), hadith, history, jurisprudence (*fiqh*), and other fields—all of which we will explore in the coming chapters, *in sha' Allah*.

### His Lineage, Birth, and Upbringing[^12]:

He is the Imam, the scholar, the master memorizer of Hadith, the exegete, and the historian: Abu al-Fida' Imad al-Din Ismail bin Umar bin Kathir bin Daw' bin Kathir bin Zar' al-Qurashi al-Busrawi[13], then al-Dimashqi, the Shafi'i jurist. He was born in Majdal, a village east of Busra in the province of Damascus, in the year 701 AH, where his father served as a preacher (*khatib*). He later moved to Damascus in the year 707 AH with his brother Kamal al-Din Abd al-Wahhab following their father's death.

Let us allow Imam Ibn Kathir himself to describe his upbringing and family when he documented his father’s biography in his book *Al-Bidayah wa al-Nihayah* under the events of the year 703 AH, writing:

> "In it passed away the father, the preacher Shihab al-Din Abu Hafs Umar bin Kathir bin Daw' bin Kathir bin Daw' bin Dar' al-Qurashi from Banu Haslah. They trace their lineage to nobility, and they possess documented genealogical records. Our teacher, Al-Mizzi, inspected some of them, found them admirable, and rejoiced over them. Because of this, he began writing my lineage as: Al-Qurashi—from a village called Al-Sharkuween, west of Busra, located between it and Adhri'at. He was born there around the year 640 AH and pursued learning under his maternal uncles, Banu Uqbah, in Busra. He studied the basics of the Hanafi school of jurisprudence, memorized *Al-Jumal* by Al-Zujaji[14], focused on Arabic grammar, morphology, and language, and memorized Arabic poetry until he could compose fine, exquisite, and eloquent poetry in praise and elegies, along with a small amount of satire. He was appointed to teaching positions in the schools of Busra at the Camel Resting Place (*Mabrak*)[15] north of the town."

> Then he moved to become the preacher of the village east of Busra, adopted the Shafi'i school of jurisprudence, and studied under Al-Nawawi[^16] and Sheikh Taj al-Din al-Fazari[^17]. The latter honored and respected him, as our Sheikh, the Allamah Ibn al-Zamalkani, informed me. He stayed there for about twelve years, then moved to the pulpit of Majdal, the village of his mother, where he resided for a long period in prosperity, sufficiency, and frequent recitation. He was an eloquent preacher who was well-received by the people, and his words carried weight due to his piety, eloquence, and sweetness of speech. He preferred staying in rural towns due to the ease he found there and the availability of lawful provisions for himself and his family.
> Several children were born to him from my mother and from another wife before her. The eldest was Ismail, then Yunus, Idris, and then from my mother: Abd al-Wahhab, Abd al-Aziz, Muhammad, and several sisters. Then I am the youngest of them all. I was named after my brother Ismail because he had come to Damascus and pursued his education there after memorizing the Qur'an with his father, studying an introductory text in grammar, memorizing *Al-Tanbih* and its commentary under the Allamah Taj al-Din al-Fazari, and acquiring *Al-Muntakhab* in the principles of jurisprudence—as our Sheikh Ibn al-Zamalkani told me.
> Afterwards, he fell from the roof of the Al-Shamiyyah al-Barraniyyah school, lingered for a few days, and passed away. My father grieved deeply over him and wrote many lines of poetry elegizing him. When I was born thereafter, he named me after him. Thus, his eldest child was Ismail, and his last and youngest child was Ismail. May Allah have mercy on those who have passed and grant a good conclusion to those who remain! My father passed away in the month of Jumada al-Ula in the year 703 AH in the village of Majdal, and was buried in its northern cemetery near the olive trees. At that time, I was a small child of three years or so, and I barely remember him except like a dream.
> Then, after him, in the year 707 AH, we moved to Damascus in the company of my brother Kamal al-Din Abd al-Wahhab, who was a full brother to us, kind, and deeply compassionate. His death occurred later in the year 750 AH. I pursued knowledge under his guidance, and Allah the Almighty facilitated what He facilitated and made easy whatever was difficult. And Allah knows best."[^18]

### His Pursuit of Knowledge:

Ibn Kathir dedicated his life to the pursuit of knowledge from an early age. He began under the guidance of his brother Abd al-Wahhab, as previously mentioned, and then turned to acquire learning from the foremost scholars of his time. He memorized the entire Qur'an, completing it in the year 711 AH[^19]. He also read portions of the Qur'an to Sheikh Muhammad bin Ja'far bin Farwush al-Labbad (who passed away in 724 AH)[^20], who had taught the Qur'an to people in the Great Mosque for about forty years, noting: "And I read a portion of the Qur'an to him"[^21].

He memorized *Al-Tanbih* by Abu Ishaq al-Shirazi (who passed away in 476 AH)[^22] and recited it from memory at the age of eighteen[^23]. He also memorized the abridgment of Ibn al-Hajib (who passed away in 646 AH)[^24]. He studied jurisprudence under two prominent scholars: Burhan al-Din al-Fazari (passed away in 729 AH) and Kamal al-Din Qadi Shuhbah (passed away in 726 AH).

Furthermore, he became the son-in-law of Al-Hafiz Abu al-Hajjaj al-Mizzi by marrying his daughter, which allowed him to closely accompany him and master the science of Hadith, including the knowledge of chains of narration, hidden defects, and biographic evaluation. He also maintained a close companionship with Sheikh Taqi al-Din Ibn Taymiyyah, enjoying a special bond with him, passionately defending him, and following his views on many matters. He notably issued legal verdicts according to Ibn Taymiyyah's opinion regarding the issue of divorce, for which he faced trial and persecution[^25].

Through these endeavors, Ibn Kathir advanced in his mastery of the Islamic and Arabic sciences until he reached the pinnacle of scholarship and became a widely recognized authority. Regarding him, Ibn Taghri Birdi[^26] stated:

> "He excelled in jurisprudence, exegesis (*tafsir*), and Hadith... He compiled, authored, taught, transmitted Hadith, and wrote books."[^27]

Al-Shawkani remarked:

> "He excelled in jurisprudence, exegesis, and grammar, and deeply investigated the narrators of Hadith and its hidden defects."[^28]

Thus, Ibn Kathir’s continuous dedication and relentless zeal in seeking and acquiring knowledge from a very tender age transformed him into a master of Hadith, a knowledgeable Imam, a sublime exegete, a capable historian, and an accomplished jurist. Another major factor contributing to the diversity and breadth of his learning was his close companionship with the great scholars of his era—such as Al-Mizzi, Al-Dhahabi, Ibn al-Zamalkani, Al-Birzali, and most notably, Ibn Taymiyyah.

In addition to this, Ibn Kathir’s approach toward the political authorities of his time was not an official or court-bound relationship. Instead, it was one of involvement focused on giving counsel, guidance, advice, teaching, resolving disputes, and similar matters whenever he was called upon by emirs and others. These figures eagerly sought his participation in such affairs and events. This type of relationship afforded Ibn Kathir sufficient freedom in his writings and works, preventing him from yielding to the suggestions or temptations of the authorities. In fact, he was sometimes tested and persecuted due to his firmness in certain positions and views—especially those in which he followed his teacher, Ibn Taymiyyah[^29].

Consequently, we find in his works abundant knowledge, profound culture, and varied insights. His books—particularly his *Tafsir* and his history (*Al-Bidayah wa al-Nihayah*)—are filled with discussions concerning the People of the Book, their deviations, the alterations that occurred in their revealed scriptures, and his dialogues with their priests and clergy. They also reflect his comprehensive grasp of the creeds of misguided sects and factions, along with his clear exposition of their deviations and errors—such as the Qadariyyah, Jahmiyyah, and Mu'tazilah—as well as the conflicts and disputes that arose between Ahl al-Sunnah and the Rafidah.

Furthermore, he possessed deep knowledge of the Tatars and their legal code, which was established by Hulagu as their religion and judicial reference. This was in addition to his acquaintance with other sciences, such as astronomy, celestial mechanics (*ilm al-hay'ah*), geography, history, and the political and social events of his time, all of which bear testimony to his vast knowledge and extensive reading.
